1. What kind of device actually fits her situation

She can still walk with a walker, but she's unsteady on it — so this isn't just "convenience mobility," it's a fall-prevention tool. That shifts the priority from range/price toward stability and safe transfers. It also rules out the two extremes:

  • Manual wheelchair — ruled out; she wants power, not something to self-propel or be pushed in.
  • Mobility scooter (tiller-steered) — ranked lowest for her specifically. Scooters have a wide turning radius (33"+), which is awkward indoors, but more importantly the transfer on/off a scooter (swinging a leg over onto a bench seat, steadying yourself on a tiller column) is harder to do safely for someone already feeling unstable than transferring into a wheelchair with fixed armrests to hold onto.
Best fit: a mid-wheel-drive power wheelchair with a captain's seat and armrests. Mid-wheel drive gives the tightest turning circle of any powered option (often 20–24") for hallways and bathrooms, while still handling sidewalk cracks and curb cuts outdoors. Just as important for her: these chairs have sturdy, often swing-away or flip-back armrests she can brace against during the walker-to-chair transfer, and a fixed, stable seat height (or a power-elevate seat that comes up to meet her).

2. Because she's feeling unstable — what to prioritize

Worth flagging to her doctor and to whoever does the ADP assessment: "feeling unstable on the walker" is itself a fall-risk symptom, separate from the wheelchair question. It's worth a specific mention — sometimes it's medication, blood pressure, vision, or inner-ear related and treatable, and the OT assessment for the chair will also formally evaluate her fall risk and transfer safety, which will shape exactly which chair and seating they recommend.

When comparing chairs, look for:

  • Swing-away or flip-back armrests — she can lower herself onto the seat using a fixed point of support, then the armrest locks in place once seated.
  • A seat belt — standard on rehab-grade chairs, easy to add if not.
  • A wide, stable wheelbase / independent suspension — the Drive Titan AXS is built so all 6 wheels stay on the ground at all times (no rocking on uneven ground); the Permobil M3's FlexLink suspension does the same job at a higher price point.
  • Power seat elevate (Jazzy Air 2, iLevel on the Q6 Edge 3) — this can actually make the transfer safer: the seat rises to meet her rather than her having to lower herself a full seat-height down, or push up out of a low seat.
  • Adjustable joystick sensitivity / low-effort joystick — worth asking the OT about if her grip strength is also reduced.

Given this, I've moved the ultra-light folding/portable chairs and the scooter down to a secondary "other options" section below — their smaller casters and higher center of gravity make them noticeably tippier, which is the opposite of what she needs right now.

6. Ontario funding (ADP + top-ups)

Ontario's Assistive Devices Program (ADP) pays 75% of the ADP-approved price of an eligible power wheelchair — rising to 100% if she's on ODSP or otherwise qualifies as low-income. It is not income-tested to start — eligibility is based on having a long-term physical disability, confirmed by an ADP-authorized assessor.
  1. Get assessed. An ADP "authorizer" — an occupational therapist or physiotherapist approved by the Ministry of Health — examines her, confirms the need, recommends the right device/seating (including any anti-tip or seat belt additions given her instability), and submits the clinical portion of the application. Several dealers above (e.g., Mobility Specialties, HME) can arrange this, or use an independent clinic:
    • Global Health Physiotherapy — 161 Eglinton Ave E, Unit 209, Toronto (near Leaside) — (416) 500-4200 — does home-visit ADP assessments, same-day booking available. OHIP doesn't cover the assessment fee directly, but supplemental/extended health benefits sometimes do. Site →
  2. Wait for approval. Typically 1–2 months from application to approval.
  3. Buy through an ADP-registered vendor (all dealers above qualify) — the vendor applies your ADP funding directly rather than you paying it all upfront and being reimbursed.

Top-up funding: March of Dimes Canada — a separate charitable Assistive Devices Program that can help cover part of the remaining 25% (or gaps ADP doesn't cover). Toll-free 1-866-765-7237, adp@marchofdimes.ca. Program info →

Note: higher-end chairs with power tilt/recline are sometimes provided through Ontario's Central Equipment Pool (new/refurbished loan pool with maintenance included) rather than an outright purchase — the ADP authorizer will flag this if it applies to her.

7. Before you buy — checklist

  • Mention the instability to her doctor, separately from the equipment search — new or worsening unsteadiness can have a treatable cause and is worth ruling out.
  • Measure her house. Narrowest interior doorway, hallway width, and turning space in the bathroom/bedroom she'd need to reach. Most mid-wheel chairs are 22–25" wide; the Q6 Stretto's narrower chassis is the fix if doorways run 28" or under.
  • Check every entrance she'd use for steps. Power wheelchairs can't climb stairs — if there's a step at the front door, she'll need a portable ramp, or the chair only solves half the indoor/outdoor goal.
  • Look at her actual sidewalks. Cracked pavement or sloped driveway aprons argue for the Permobil/Titan AXS tier's stronger suspension over a base model.
  • Ask the OT specifically about transfer technique — armrest height, which side she transfers from, and whether a seat belt or extra bracing is needed.
  • Ask for a test drive or in-home trial before committing — HME's showroom allows walk-in test drives with no appointment; several dealers offer short-term rental (~$300–500/month for power chairs GTA-wide) as a trial period.
  • Confirm local service/repair — Mobility Specialties, HME, and Vital Mobility all do local repairs and battery replacement.
