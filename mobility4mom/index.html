1. What kind of device fits the requirements

The core requirement is a device that works both outdoors on sidewalks and indoors in the house. That rules out the two extremes:

  • Manual wheelchair — ruled out; the requirement is power, not something to self-propel or be pushed in.
  • Mobility scooter (tiller-steered) — good outdoor range, but a wide turning radius (33"+) and long wheelbase make it awkward in tight hallways, bathrooms, and bedroom doorways.
Best fit: a mid-wheel-drive power wheelchair. Mid-wheel drive gives the tightest turning circle of any powered option (often 20–24") — good for kitchens, bathrooms, and hallways — while still handling sidewalk cracks, driveway aprons, and curb cuts outdoors. This is the category the shortlist below focuses on, with portable/folding and scooter alternatives listed separately.

5. Ontario funding (ADP + top-ups)

Ontario's Assistive Devices Program (ADP) pays 75% of the ADP-approved price of an eligible power wheelchair — rising to 100% if she's on ODSP or otherwise qualifies as low-income. It is not income-tested to start — eligibility is based on having a long-term physical disability, confirmed by an ADP-authorized assessor.
  1. Get assessed. An ADP "authorizer" — an occupational therapist or physiotherapist approved by the Ministry of Health — examines her, confirms the long-term need, recommends the right device/seating, and submits the clinical portion of the application. Several dealers above (e.g., Mobility Specialties, HME) can arrange this, or use an independent clinic:
    • Global Health Physiotherapy — 161 Eglinton Ave E, Unit 209, Toronto (near Leaside) — (416) 500-4200 — does home-visit ADP assessments, same-day booking available. OHIP doesn't cover the assessment fee directly, but supplemental/extended health benefits sometimes do. Site →
  2. Wait for approval. Typically 1–2 months from application to approval.
  3. Buy through an ADP-registered vendor (all dealers above qualify) — the vendor applies your ADP funding directly rather than you paying it all upfront and being reimbursed.

Top-up funding: March of Dimes Canada — a separate charitable Assistive Devices Program that can help cover part of the remaining 25% (or gaps ADP doesn't cover). Toll-free 1-866-765-7237, adp@marchofdimes.ca. Program info →

Note: higher-end chairs with power tilt/recline are sometimes provided through Ontario's Central Equipment Pool (new/refurbished loan pool with maintenance included) rather than an outright purchase — the ADP authorizer will flag this if it applies.

6. Before you buy — checklist

  • Measure the house. Narrowest interior doorway, hallway width, and turning space in the bathroom/bedroom she'd need to reach. Most mid-wheel chairs are 22–25" wide; older Toronto homes sometimes have 28" doorways, which is where the Quantum Stretto's narrower chassis matters.
  • Check every entrance for steps. Power wheelchairs can't climb stairs — if there's a step at the front door, a portable ramp will be needed, or the chair only solves the indoor half of the requirement.
  • Look at the actual sidewalks. Cracked pavement, sloped driveway aprons, or hills nearby argue for the better suspension on the Permobil/Quantum tier over a base model.
  • Decide if car transport matters — if yes, that pushes toward the portable/folding category even though range and outdoor ride suffer a bit.
  • Start the ADP assessment early — it takes 4–8 weeks and the OT's in-home visit will also confirm which model fits the house and body, so it's worth doing before locking in a specific chair.
  • Ask for a test drive or in-home trial before committing — HME's showroom allows walk-in test drives with no appointment; several dealers offer short-term rental (~$300–500/month for power chairs GTA-wide) as a trial period.
  • Confirm local service/repair — Mobility Specialties, HME, and Vital Mobility all do local repairs and battery replacement, which matters more than the sticker price over a few years of ownership.
